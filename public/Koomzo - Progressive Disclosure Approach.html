Progressive disclosure as product architecture

Koomzo Suite · UX approach

A single codebase that presents itself as a different product to a one-person kiosk and to a twelve-store chain — without forking the product, splitting the SKU, or asking the small merchant to grow into an interface they did not order.

01The problem this solves

Vertical software fails at both ends of the market, usually for opposite reasons.

Built for the large customer

The small merchant meets a shift-reconciliation screen on day one, cannot find the Charge button behind four back-office tabs, and abandons during trial. The product is judged complicated before it is judged capable.

Built for the small customer

The growing merchant hits a ceiling — no purchase orders, no multi-location, no accountability trail — and migrates to a competitor at exactly the moment their account became valuable. Churn arrives disguised as success.

The industry's usual answer is editions: separate builds, separate price cards, separate codebases that drift apart. Editions solve the pricing problem and create an engineering one. Every feature must be written twice, tested twice, and documented twice; upgrading a customer is a migration project.

Koomzo's position: one product, one data model, one build — and a configurable surface. Capability is always present. Only its visibility is configured.

02The principle

1
Capability is universal; surface is configured
Every install ships every feature. Setup decides which screens, fields and steps the operator ever meets. Nothing is compiled out, licensed separately, or absent from the schema.
2
Hiding is never destructive
Turning a capability off hides its UI and stops its prompts. It does not delete records, break references, or rewrite history. Turning it back on reveals data that was accumulating all along.
3
A named tier is a starting point, not a cage
Tiers seed a configuration. The merchant then adjusts any single switch. A configuration that matches no tier is legitimate and labelled Custom — never an error state.
4
One core is never optional
Each module has an irreducible spine that cannot be hidden — the register in POS, items in Inventory, the appointment book in Salon. It guarantees the app is always operable, whatever else is off.
5
Off means off everywhere
A hidden capability disappears from the rail, the ticket, the search results, the reports, the keyboard shortcuts and the onboarding checklist. A single hidden screen leaking one orphaned button is the failure mode that destroys trust in the whole mechanism.
6
The escalation is visible, not hidden
When a merchant needs something that is switched off, the empty state names the capability and offers the switch — not a support ticket. Discovery is designed; it does not depend on curiosity.
7
Configuration is an admin act, not a user act
Setup lives behind a role. A cashier cannot simplify their own till, and cannot complicate it either. Configuration is stable across a shift.

03The four-tier ladder

Every module expresses the same ladder. The names are constant across the suite so a merchant who learns it once in POS recognises it in Inventory, Salon and Restaurant.

Tier 0OffThe module is not part of this business. It is absent from navigation entirely.Not applicable
Tier 1LiteOne screen, the irreducible job. No documents, no approvals, no reconciliation.Owner-operator
Tier 2MidThe daily loop with accountability: who did it, when, and against what record.Small team, one site
Tier 3FullDocuments, multi-site, approvals, valuation, analytics. Every switch available.Multi-site operator
The tier is a preset over the same switch matrix, not a separate build. Selecting Mid writes a set of booleans; the merchant is then free to turn one of them off and land on Custom. This is why the ladder costs nothing to maintain.

Two cross-cutting layers ride the same mechanism and must be built once, centrally, rather than per module:

  • Role visibility — a capability may be on for the business but off for this role. Roles subtract; they never add.
  • Device visibility — a capability may be on for the business but irrelevant on this device. A kitchen tablet and a back-office laptop show different subsets of an identical configuration.
Resolution order matters and must be one function. Effective visibility = module tier → capability switch → role → device, each able only to subtract. Implemented in one place, every screen agrees; implemented per screen, the orphaned-button bug returns forever.

05Why this is a commercial position, not just a UX one

Time to first value

A merchant can be selling in minutes because the interface they meet contains nothing they must first understand. Trial-to-paid is decided in the first session, and the first session is the one we control most tightly.

Training cost falls out of the product

Training scope equals visible surface. A Lite install has almost nothing to teach; onboarding is a walkthrough, not a course. Support volume tracks surface area, not licence count.

Expansion instead of migration

Growth is a toggle, not a project — no export, no re-keying, no data loss, no second contract. The upgrade conversation is about value, not risk.

One codebase, one truth

Features are written, tested and documented once. Tiers are data. The engineering cost of serving four market segments is the cost of a switch matrix.

Packaging stays flexible

Because tiers are data, commercial packaging can be re-cut — by tier, by module, by seat — without an engineering release.

The demo tells the truth

Sales can show the exact surface a given prospect will get, then reveal the ceiling above it in the same session. No mock environments, no "that comes in Enterprise".

06Design obligations

Progressive disclosure fails in predictable ways. These are the obligations that prevent each one.

No orphaned affordances
Every button, badge, filter and menu entry declares which capability it depends on. If that capability is off, the affordance is not rendered — not disabled, not greyed, not present.
Redirect, never dead-end
If the operator is standing on a screen that becomes hidden, they land on the module core, not on a blank frame or an error.
Setup is searchable and reversible
A merchant looking for "returns" must find the switch by that word. Every switch shows its consequence in plain language before it is flipped, and can be flipped back.
Empty states sell the next tier
"This register sells from a fixed product list — enable Lite when you need quantities" teaches the ladder at the exact moment of need. Every off capability owns one such state.
Vocabulary is constant
Off / Lite / Mid / Full mean the same thing in every module. A capability keeps its name in Setup, in the rail, in the docs and in the invoice.
Changes are logged
Configuration is auditable: who turned what on, and when. It explains why last month's report has a column this month's does not.

07Rollout plan

Retail POS and Inventory already implement this. The work is to extract the mechanism, then apply it module by module.

Phase 1

Extract the capability service

One shared registry: capability keys, human names, descriptions, dependencies, tier presets, and the single resolver that folds tier → switch → role → device into one visibility answer. Everything downstream reads it; nothing re-implements it. Ship with the existing POS and Inventory maps as its first two consumers.

Phase 2

Standardise the Setup surface

One Setup pattern reused by every module: tier picker, searchable capability list with plain-language consequences, and a live preview of the resulting navigation. Merchants configure a fifth module the way they configured the first.

Phase 3

Audit every module for orphans

Mechanical sweep: each interactive element declares its capability dependency or is explicitly marked core. This is where the mechanism earns trust, and where it is cheapest to get wrong.

Phase 4

Salon, Restaurant, Queue, Invoicing

Apply the ladder module by module, in that order — Salon first because its Lite case (a single stylist) is the largest untapped segment and the clearest test of the model.

Phase 5

Onboarding derived from configuration

The setup wizard asks about the business, not the software — headcount, sites, whether returns are accepted, whether stock is counted — and writes the switch matrix. The resulting checklist covers only visible surface, so it is always short and always finishable.

Phase 6

Instrument and tune the ladder

Measure which capabilities merchants turn on, in what order, and how long after signup. That data re-cuts the tier presets — the ladder should reflect observed behaviour, not our original guess.

08Open questions — resolved

All five are now answered in the companion document, with mechanics and the engineering consequences of each choice.

Read the decisions
  • Tier naming in the commercial layer. Does Lite / Mid / Full appear on the price card, or stay an internal shape with pricing cut by module and seat?
  • Who may configure. Owner-only, or a delegated manager role — and is Setup available on a phone, or deliberately desktop-only to keep it a considered act?
  • Downgrade behaviour. Hiding a capability that already holds records — open purchase orders, an unreconciled shift — needs a defined resolution, not a silent hide.
  • Reporting continuity. When a capability is turned on mid-period, reports gain a dimension mid-series. Do we backfill, annotate, or segment?
  • Default tier at signup. Start everyone at Lite and let them climb, or infer a tier from the signup questionnaire and risk starting too complex?
