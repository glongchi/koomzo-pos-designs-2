MRP — information architecture & Central African fit

A manufacturing tier for Koomzo, designed for the plants that actually exist between Douala and Yaoundé: agro-processing and packing operations where yield, import lead time and power availability decide the schedule. Same data model as Inventory — one catalogue, one quantity, one purchasing queue.

The one design decision

MRP is not a second system bolted beside stock — it is three new objects on top of the Inventory model.

1
Part = the Item you already have
A cocoa bean, a jar, a WIP tote of liquor and a finished spread jar are all Items with a quantity. MRP adds a kind (raw / wip / packaging / finished) and a source (local / import). Nothing is duplicated, so a finished jar can carry Show in POS the day it comes off the line.
2
BOM = an Item that knows how it is made
Components, a yield, and optional co-products. This is the difference between a retail recipe and a process plant: 1 000 kg of beans leaves as 800 kg of nibs plus 180 kg of shell, so the BOM plans the input and credits the co-product before unit cost.
3
Work order = the document that moves stock
Like a purchase order or a transfer, it posts movements onto the same stock card the store sees. Output, scrap, lot, material picked, energy hours — all captured against the order, which is what makes job costing possible without a second ledger.
4
Planning run reads demand, writes suggestions
Firm contracts plus forecast, netted against on-hand, allocated and incoming. It never posts anything: it produces suggested purchases that drop into the existing purchase-order screen and suggested work orders a planner approves.
Nothing new in the register. POS keeps reading the item channel layer built for Inventory. A manufactured good is visible at the till because its Item says so — not because MRP pushed anything.

Tiers

Off / Lite / Full, exactly as Inventory does it. Same tables at every tier, so a workshop that grows into a plant migrates nothing.

Built for the Central African constraint set

Four things about a Douala plant that generic MRP UIs handle badly, and how these screens answer them.

Import lead time is the real planning parameter

Every part carries a supplier lead time plus a port buffer (18 d Douala, 14 d Kribi). The plan shows landed lead time against the need date and says so out loud when it cannot make it — with the three real options: air-freight the gap, cut the batch, substitute a local grade.

Power decides the schedule, so it is on screen

Work centres declare grid / generator tolerance and the day is drawn as a grid window and a generator window. Generator hours cost about 38% more, so the extra lands on the job as an energy variance rather than disappearing into monthly overhead.

Connectivity drops, work does not

The floor phone stamps the lot locally and queues every action. Offline is a permanent chip and a banner with a count, never a toast that vanishes — an operator must be able to trust that a shift of logging is not lost.

Yield variance is the margin

Bean moisture, press residual fat, over-dosed sugar: each is a fraction of a percent that decides whether a run made money. Actual yield sits beside standard on every closed job, with the reason written in plain language.

Who this fits in Cameroon

From your sector list, the shortlist where this specific design lands — process plants with recipes, yields, lots and import exposure — rather than heavy discrete industry.

Strong fit today

Cocoa transformation and confectionery · soap, detergent and cosmetics blending · juice, water and beverage bottling · dairy and bouillon-style food processing · bakery and flour-based groups · palm-oil and shea processing · plastics with resin/regrind ratios.

All share: a recipe with a yield, co-products, lot traceability for an export buyer, and imported inputs with long landed lead times.

Needs more than this tier

Steel and aluminium (multi-level WIP through rolling mills, furnace scheduling) · sawmilling and veneer (log-to-grade conversion with per-log variability, kiln cycles, FLEGT documents) · cement (continuous process with spares-driven maintenance) · textiles (loom scheduling, dye-lot management).

Each needs one heavier subsystem — finite scheduling, grade conversion, or CMMS — before it is honest to sell.

Rollout shape

1
Weeks 1–2 · Parts and BOMs
Load the catalogue with kinds and units, then the recipes with real yields taken from last quarter's actuals — not the design spec. Yield accuracy is what makes every later number credible.
2
Weeks 3–4 · Lite live on the floor
Work orders and output logging only, on phones, in French. One line, one shift, one supervisor. The metric is whether logging survives a power cut, not whether the plan is clever.
3
Weeks 5–8 · Full tier
Turn on the planning run once supplier lead times and port buffers are entered honestly. Quality gates and traceability follow, driven by whichever export buyer audits first.
4
Quarter 2 · Costing review rhythm
A weekly variance meeting on four numbers: material, energy, scrap, yield. Nothing else in the module changes behaviour as fast.
Open questions worth deciding before build. Subcontracting (send liquor out for pressing) is modelled in the capability list but has no screen yet. Preventive maintenance is deliberately absent. And costing currently rolls standard vs actual per job — a moving-average valuation for OHADA reporting is a separate decision with the accountant.
